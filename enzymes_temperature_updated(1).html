What is an enzyme?

Enzymes (E) are biological catalysts that speed up the chemical reactions keeping you alive.

Without them, vital processes like digesting food and making energy would happen far too slowly for life to exist.

They turn substrates (S) into products (P) by lowering the activation energy (Ea), without changing the reaction’s overall energy (ΔG).

They bond because positives (enzyme) and negatives (substrate) attract.

E + SEnzyme meets substrate
⇌
ESThey bond
→
E + PEnzyme is free, product is made

Why enzymes are important

Enzymes make the chemical reactions needed for life happen quickly enough and under the mild conditions inside living cells.

They speed up reactions

Enzymes lower the activation energy needed for a reaction. This means reactions can happen faster without needing high temperatures.

They help release energy

Digestive enzymes break large food molecules into smaller nutrients. Cells can then use these nutrients in chemical reactions, including cellular respiration, to release energy.

They protect cells

Enzymes such as catalase break down harmful substances made inside cells. Other enzymes help important processes, such as copying DNA, take place.

Why this matters

Without enzymes, many reactions in the body would happen too slowly to keep us alive. Enzymes are specific: each active site fits certain substrates, and the enzyme can be used again after the reaction.

Example: amylase in saliva

Amylase speeds up the breakdown of starch into smaller sugars, such as maltose. This is one of the first steps in digesting carbohydrates.

Starch + water — amylase → smaller sugars

Enzymes we learned about

Three digestive enzymes, each with its own job.

Lipase

Breaks down fats into fatty acids and glycerol.

Amylase

Breaks down starch into smaller sugars.

Protease

Breaks down proteins into smaller peptides and amino acids.

Three enzymes in more detail

These enzymes have different jobs in digestion and protection.

Pepsin

Pepsin is a digestive enzyme found in the stomach. It breaks large protein molecules into smaller pieces called peptides, making them easier for the body to digest further.

It works best in the acidic conditions of the stomach. Pepsin is first made in an inactive form and becomes active when stomach acid is present. A protective mucus layer containing bicarbonate helps stop the stomach lining from being damaged by the acid and pepsin.

The active site is the part where the substrate binds.

Catalase

Catalase is found in almost all living cells, including liver cells. Its job is to break down hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a harmful substance that can form during reactions inside cells.

It converts hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. This helps protect cells from damage. Catalase works extremely quickly: one enzyme molecule can break down millions of hydrogen peroxide molecules in a second.

2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2

The folded shape creates a specific active site.

Lysozyme

Lysozyme is an enzyme that helps protect the body against bacteria. It is found in fluids such as tears and saliva, which are part of the body's first line of defence.

It attacks a material in bacterial cell walls called peptidoglycan. By breaking bonds in this material, lysozyme weakens the cell wall. This makes it harder for the bacteria to survive, helping the body prevent infections.

Lysozyme's active site binds to parts of the bacterial cell wall.

Temperature and enzyme activity

Change the temperature to see how it affects an enzyme. Most human enzymes work best around 37°C.

37°C
High enzyme activity

At the optimum temperature, the active site has the right shape and the reaction works quickly.

Normal enzyme shape

The substrate can fit into the active site.

At very high temperatures, bonds holding the enzyme's shape can change. The active site loses its shape, so the enzyme becomes denatured.
